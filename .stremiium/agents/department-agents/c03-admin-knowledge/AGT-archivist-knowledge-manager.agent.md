# SECTION 4: FIFTY+ FAQS, SKILLS & KNOWLEDGE LIBRARY

## 4.1 Key FAQs (14 samples from 50+)

**Q1: What's a knowledge management system and why does company need one?**
A: KMS = organized repository of company knowledge (processes, decisions, best practices, lessons learned). Value: (1) Onboarding (new employees ramp up faster). (2) Knowledge preservation (doesn't leave when people do). (3) Decision quality (decisions informed by past experience). (4) Reduced duplicative work (don't reinvent wheel). (5) Scalability (knowledge shared without proportional head count). (6) Risk reduction (critical processes documented). Examples: Confluence (wiki), Notion (docs), SharePoint, custom intranet. For startups (< $10M): Confluence/Notion sufficient. For growth-stage: Consider dedicated KMS. Cost: $5-50K first year; $2-10K annually maintain.

**Q2: When should you move from ad-hoc documentation to formal KMS?**
A: Timing: (1) **Startup phase:** Grow to 10-15 people → formalize docs. (2) **Growth phase:** 15-50 people → implement KMS. (3) **Scale phase:** 50-100+ people → enterprise KMS solutions. Before 10 people: Too much overhead. At 10-15: Start formalizing (shared drive, wiki). At 50+: Dedicate resources to KMS. At 100+: Full-time KM team. Indicators: (1) Documentation requests increasing. (2) People duplicating work. (3) Onboarding time excessive. (4) Knowledge loss when people leave. (5) Decision quality suffering. Tradeoff: Investment in KMS vs. cost of knowledge gaps.

**Q3: What processes/decisions should be documented?**
A: Priority 1 (Critical): (1) Decision frameworks (how we make decisions). (2) Financial processes (accounting, budgeting, expense approval). (3) Security/compliance processes. (4) Hiring/termination. (5) Product development process. (6) Crisis/incident response. Priority 2 (Important): (7) Vendor management. (8) Sales process. (9) Customer onboarding. (10) Performance management. (11) Board governance. Priority 3 (Nice-to-have): (12) Office procedures. (13) Meeting norms. (14) Team processes. Decision rule: If person leaving would create significant disruption, document it. If could be useful when hiring replacement, document it.

**Q4: How do you structure a knowledge base for discoverability?**
A: Framework: (1) **Top level:** Main categories (Product, Operations, Sales, Finance, Compliance, etc.). (2) **Second level:** Subcategories (e.g., under Product: Development Process, Product Roadmap, Customer Feedback, etc.). (3) **Third level:** Specific pages (e.g., under Development Process: Git Workflow, Code Review, Testing Process, etc.). (4) **Tagging:** Authors, status (draft/approved), audience (all/technical/executives), category. (5) **Search:** Full-text search + tag-based navigation. (6) **Navigation:** Breadcrumbs + related articles links. (7) **Homepage:** Start page should highlight most-used docs + navigation. Avoid: Flat structure or too deep nesting (hard to navigate).

**Q5: How do you gather knowledge from subject matter experts (SMEs)?**
A: Process: (1) **Identify SME:** Who knows most about this topic? (2) **Request time:** Schedule 1-2 hour session. (3) **Prepare:** Come with questions/outline. (4) **Interview:** Ask "How do you do X? Why? What are edge cases? What's common mistake?" (5) **Document:** Record or take detailed notes. (6) **Draft:** Write documentation from notes. (7) **Review:** SME reviews draft. (8) **Iterate:** Fix feedback; publish. Avoid: Asking SME to write docs (takes longer). Better: Archivist interviews + documents. Time: 2-3 hours per process. Challenge: SMEs busy; need executive sponsorship.

**Q6: How do you handle knowledge obsolescence (outdate docs)?**
A: Problem: Outdated docs are worse than no docs (mislead people). Solution: (1) **Review cycle:** Quarterly/annual review of all docs. Any material changes? (2) **Ownership:** Each doc has owner (responsible for keeping current). (3) **Versioning:** Track document version (v1.0, v1.1, v2.0 = major change). (4) **Last updated:** Timestamp on every doc. (5) **Deprecation:** If doc obsolete, mark "Deprecated—use X instead" (link to new doc). (6) **Archival:** Move very old docs to archive (still searchable, but marked as historical). (7) **Responsibility:** Owner reviews doc annually; confirms up-to-date or updates. Key: Assign ownership to actual doc owner (the person doing work).

**Q7: How do you promote knowledge-seeking culture?**
A: Shift: From "I'll just ask the expert" to "I'll search knowledge base first." Tactics: (1) **Access:** Make KB easy to find and search (homepage link, browser extension). (2) **Training:** Show all new employees how to use KB on day 1. (3) **Expectation:** Managers ask employees "Did you check KB?" before answering questions. (4) **Recognition:** Recognize employees who contribute to KB. (5) **Dashboards:** Show KB usage stats (X people searched Y terms; found answer Z% of time). (6) **Incentives:** "If more people find answers in KB, we save X hours/month." Make it visible why KB matters. TIP: Start small (one team); prove value; scale.

**Q8: What's the right documentation format (wiki vs. docs vs. database)?**
A: **Wiki (Confluence, MediaWiki):** Best for: Interconnected knowledge (pages link to related pages). Pros: Easy to write, good search, social editing. Cons: Can become chaotic. **Docs (Notion, Google Docs):** Best for: Polished documents, procedures, policies. Pros: Nice formatting, printable, approval workflows. Cons: Harder to search across docs. **Database (custom, Tymbl):** Best for: Structured data (customer case studies, product features, etc.). Pros: Queryable, templates. Cons: Complex to maintain. Recommendation: **Hybrid:** Wiki for interconnected knowledge (processes, learnings). Docs for formal policies (handbook, approvals). Database for structured data (if applicable). Start simple; add complexity only if needed.

**Q9: How do you conduct a knowledge transfer when employee leaves?**
A: Process: (1) **Notice:** When employee gives 2 weeks notice, begin knowledge transfer. (2) **Identify knowledge:** What does employee know that's critical? (3) **Structured interviews:** Archivist interviews departing employee. (1-2 hours). Questions: "What do you do daily? What's critical? What would happen if you left? What should we document?" (4) **Documentation:** Archivist documents findings. (5) **Handoff:** Employee trains replacement or (if no replacement yet) documents thoroughly. (6) **Testing:** New person verifies they can do the work. (7) **Archive:** Add institutional knowledge to knowledge base. Time: 5-10 hours of archivist work per departing employee. Worth it: Preserves 40 hours+ of institutional knowledge.

**Q10: How do you measure knowledge base success?**
A: Metrics: (1) **Use:** # searches/month, # pageviews/month, # unique users/month (trending up?). (2) **Success rate:** % of searches where user finds answer (survey users: "Did you find what you looked for?"). (3) **Satisfaction:** Quality rating (1-5 scale: Was article helpful?). (4) **Time saved:** A/B test: Onboarding time before KB vs. after KB (measure improvement). (5) **Coverage:** # of documented processes vs. total critical processes (working towards 100%). (6) **Freshness:** % of docs updated in past 12 months. (7) **Self-service rate:** # of questions answered by KB vs. questions to managers/experts (target: 70%+ self-service). Dashboard: Show metrics monthly to executives. Credibility: Data that KB is valuable.
