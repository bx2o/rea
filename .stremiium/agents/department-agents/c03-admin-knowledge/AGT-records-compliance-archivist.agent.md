# SECTION 4: FIFTY+ FAQS, SKILLS & KNOWLEDGE LIBRARY

## 4.1 Fifty+ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### **Category 1: RECORDS RETENTION & GOVERNANCE (14 FAQs)**

**Q1: What's a records retention policy and what should it cover?**
A: Policy: Document governing what records you keep and how long. Should cover: (1) **Record types:** Financial records, HR records, legal documents, email, etc. (2) **Retention periods:** Finance (7 years typical), HR (3-7 years), email (2-3 years), product docs (lifetime of product + X years). (3) **Storage:** Where kept? Active folder? Archive? Destroyed? (4) **Access:** Who can access? (5) **Legal holds:** If litigation, override retention policy (keep longer). (6) **Disposal:** How to safely destroy? (7) **Exceptions:** Any records exempt? Example: Board meeting minutes kept 20 years. Develop policy with Legal, Finance, HR input.

**Q2: How long should you keep different types of records?**
A: Typical retention periods: (1) **Financial:** Tax records, payroll, expenses keep 7 years (tax statute of limitations). (2) **HR:** Personnel files, performance reviews, termination docs keep 5 years. (3) **Legal:** Contracts, agreements keep 7+ years (statute of limitations). (4) **Email:** General email 2 years; executive email 7+ years (Sarbanes-Oxley for public companies). (5) **Meeting minutes:** Important decisions → 20+ years; routine → 3 years. (6) **Customer records:** If regulated, may require longer. (7) **Product docs:** Keep while product active + 5+ years. Consult Legal; varies by industry/regulation.

**Q3: What's the difference between Active records, Archive, and Disposal?**
A: **Active:** Currently needed for business (recent contracts, this year's financials). Location: Central drive, accessible. Retention: Keep until policy period ends. **Archive:** Rarely needed but must keep per policy (old contracts, historical records). Location: Cold storage (cheaper, slower to access). Retention: Keep until policy period ends. **Disposal:** Policy period expired; no longer required (old emails, outdated docs). Action: Securely destroy (delete, shred, etc.). Proper disposal = legal compliance + security.

**Q4: How do you classify records?**
A: Approach: (1) **By type:** Financial, HR, Legal, Operational, Customer, Product, Strategic. (2) **By function:** By department/team. (3) **By compliance:** What regulatory requirement? (4) **By confidentiality:** Public? Internal? Confidential? Secret? (5) **By sensitivity:** Who can access? Use metadata: Document type, owner, confidentiality level, retention period. Tools: Folder structure, tags, file naming convention. Example: Folder structure = /Records/Finance/2024-Payroll → clearly financial, 2024, payroll. Classification allows consistent retention + access control.

**Q5: What's a legal hold and when do you invoke it?**
A: Legal hold: Preserve records that might be needed for litigation. When: (1) Legal claim appears likely. (2) Litigation filed. (3) Legal counsel advises. Effect: Override normal retention policy (keep records indefinitely until hold lifted). Process: (1) General Counsel issues legal hold notice. (2) Employees must preserve records (don't delete, even if normally would). (3) Central archive preserves copies. Document what's subject to hold. Lift hold once litigation resolves.

**Q6: How do you organize records for easy retrieval?**
A: Approach: (1) **Folder structure:** Logical hierarchy (e.g., /Year/Department/Record Type or /Record Type/Year). (2) **File naming:** Consistent format (e.g., 2024-03-Payroll-March or Contracts-2024-Acme-Inc). (3) **Metadata:** Tags/fields (date, owner, type, confidentiality). (4) **Index:** If many records, consider searchable index. (5) **Access:** Clear who can access what (permissions). (6) **Tools:** Drives + tags work for some; larger org → records management system (OpenKM, Alfresco). Spend time upfront on organization; saves hours in retrieval.

**Q7: What's the difference between a records management system and a file server?**
A: **File server:** Basic folder structure, not designed for records. Challenges: No retention policies enforced, no metadata, can't search well, no audit trail. **Records management system (RMS):** Designed for records. Features: Retention policies enforced, metadata required, full-text search, audit trail (who accessed?), disposition workflow (archive/destroy). Examples: OpenKM, Alfresco, SharePoint (with governance). Small company: File server + manual process. Growth: Consider RMS. Cost: RMS $100-500/month.

**Q8: How do you securely dispose of/destroy records?**
A: Approach depends on format: (1) **Paper:** Shred (professional shredder), burn, or incinerate. Don't just throw away (dumpster diving). (2) **Digital:** Delete from system (not recoverable); for sensitive, use secure erase tool (overwrites data). (3) **Physical media:** Hard drives - use degausser or destroy. Tapes - incinerate. (4) **Cloud:** Delete from cloud provider; verify deletion. (5) **Vendor records:** If using third party for storage, ensure they destroy properly (contract requirement). Document disposal: Certificate of destruction for compliance proof.

**Q9: How do you handle email as records?**
A: Challenge: Email usually not retained long (too much volume). Approach: (1) **Selective:** Keep important emails (decisions, agreements, approvals). (2) **Archive:** Move old email to archive (usually automatic, set to 2-3 years). (3) **Policy:** Policy should cover email scope. (4) **Legal hold:** If litigation, must preserve email. (5) **Executive email:** Usually kept longer (7+ years). (6) **Tools:** Email management platforms can automate archival/retention. (7) **Cleanup:** Delete old, personal emails (not company records). Recommendation: Let most email delete at 2-3 years; selectively keep important decisions.

**Q10: How do you maintain institutional memory?**
A: Approach: (1) **Decision logs:** Document important decisions (what? why? by whom? when?). (2) **Meeting minutes:** Keep minutes of key meetings (strategic decisions, Board). (3) **Retrospectives:** When projects end, capture learnings. (4) **History documentation:** Periodically document how company evolved (timeline, key milestones). (5) **Interviews:** Before long-tenured employees leave, interview them (capture knowledge). (6) **Stories:** Record important company stories/culture. (7) **Archive:** Preserve these in accessible archive (not just lost in old folders). Purpose: Future employees/leaders understand company history + decisions.

**Q11: What's an audit trail and why do you need one?**
A: Audit trail: Log of who accessed/modified what records, when. Why: (1) **Compliance:** Regulatory requirement (healthcare, finance). (2) **Security:** Detect unauthorized access. (3) **Accountability:** Know who touched sensitive records. (4) **Investigation:** If issue, can trace what happened. Requires: Records management system (most file servers don't track). Elements: User, action (view/edit/delete), timestamp, record. Example: "Alice accessed Payroll-2024-March on Feb 12, 9:45 AM." Larger orgs: Essential; smaller: Lower priority.

**Q12: How do you handle records for remote/hybrid teams?**
A: Challenges: Records not all in office; harder to ensure centralized. Approach: (1) **Centralized system:** All records go to central location (not local drives). (2) **Cloud storage:** Use centralized cloud (Google Drive, OneDrive, Sharepoint). (3) **Policy:** Require people put records in right place. (4) **Access:** Ensure access regardless of location. (5) **Backup:** Ensure backup handles distributed access. (6) **Training:** Remote employees need training on records management. Recommendation: Centralized system is essential for remote; harder to manage if records on personal computers.

**Q13: What's a records audit and how often?**
A: Audit: Review records management process. Steps: (1) **Sample records:** Check if stored correctly. (2) **Compliance:** Are retention policies followed? (3) **Access:** Check access controls (who can access?). (4) **Disposal:** Check if disposal done properly. (5) **Audit trail:** Check logs (access tracked?). Frequency: Annual minimum; larger org → semi-annual. Effort: 20-40 hours. Cost: Can do internally or hire external auditor. Value: Ensures compliance + identifies gaps.

**Q14: How do you prepare records for litigation/audit?**
A: When lawsuit/audit coming: (1) **Legal hold:** Stop normal disposal (preserve records). (2) **Identify relevant records:** What records related to claim/audit? (3) **Organize:** Pull into central location, organized. (4) **Metadata:** Ensure we know date, author, relevance. (5) **Responsive documents:** Identify which documents responsive to request. (6) **Privilege review:** With Legal, identify attorney-client privileged docs (don't produce). (7) **Production:** Produce documents to opposing party/auditor. Timeline: 2-4 weeks typically. Benefit: Having organized records = faster response, less stressful.
