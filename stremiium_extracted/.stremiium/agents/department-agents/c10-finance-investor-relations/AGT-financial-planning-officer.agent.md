# SECTION 4: FIFTY+ FAQs

**Q1: How do you build a financial model?**
A: Steps: (1) **Revenue model:** Project revenue (by segment/channel). Start with traction + growth assumptions. (2) **COGS:** Estimate cost of goods sold (% of revenue or per-unit). (3) **Operating expenses:** Salaries, marketing, overhead. (4) **CapEx:** One-time equipment purchases. (5) **Working capital:** Changes in AR/AP (cash tied up). (6) **Assumptions:** Document key assumptions (growth rate, conversion rate, ACV). (7) **Sensitivity:** How does model change if assumptions off? (8) **Scenarios:** Build optimistic/base/pessimistic versions. Tools: Excel, Google Sheets (simple) or Anaplan, Adaptive Insights (complex). Frequency: Update monthly.

**Q2: What's a reasonable revenue growth assumption?**
A: Framework: (1) **Early stage (<$1M):** 50-200% YoY (from small base). (2) **Growth stage ($1-20M):** 30-100% YoY. (3) **Scale stage ($20M+):** 20-50% YoY. (4) **Public/mature:** 10-30% YoY. Factors: (1) Market size (TAM growing?). (2) Competitive position (gaining/losing share?). (3) Maturity (early = high growth; mature = low). (4) Sales efficiency (sales growing faster than spend?). Rule: Conservative assumption = more believable. Investors will challenge aggressive assumptions.

**Q3: What's unit economics and why does it matter?**
A: Unit economics = profitability per customer unit. Metrics: (1) **CAC:** Cost to acquire customer ($5K for enterprise customer). (2) **LTV:** Lifetime revenue from customer ($50K over 5 years). (3) **Ratio:** LTV ÷ CAC = multiple (healthy = 3:1 or higher). Example: CAC $5K, LTV $20K = 4x multiple (healthy). Why: If LTV < CAC, you lose money on each customer (unsustainable). If LTV > 3x CAC, you make good margin. Focus: Improving LTV or reducing CAC to improve unit economics.

**Q4: What's a rolling forecast?**
A: Approach: (1) **Monthly forecasts:** Forecast next 12-24 months (rolling). (2) **Update monthly:** Drop oldest month, add new future month. (3) **Year-end:** Always have 12 months ahead forecasted. Why: Better than static annual budget (assumptions change; rolling forecast adapts). Example: January forecast next 12 months (Jan-Dec). February forecast drops January results, adds January next year forecast (Feb-Jan next year). Advantage: Always planning ahead + capturing assumption changes.

**Q5: How do you analyze variances?**
A: Process: (1) **Identify variance:** Actual ≠ Forecast. How much difference? (2) **Investigate:** Why difference? (volume down? price up? new expenses?). (3) **Categorize:** Is variance explainable (one-time item) or recurring? (4) **Correct:** Need to update forecast? (5) **Report:** Explain variance to leadership. Example: Forecast $1M revenue, actual $900K. Variance = -$100K (-10%). Why: New customer delayed signing (verbal commitment but no contract). Action: Update forecast; expect $1.1M next month when deal closes.
